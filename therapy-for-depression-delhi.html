What counselling for low mood can help with in Delhi

You don’t need a diagnosis or a reason that looks big enough from outside. These are the things people bring when low mood has been weighing on them:

🌁
Loss of interest

Things you used to enjoy going flat — hobbies, people, plans. Often the earliest and most honest sign.

😶
Exhaustion & sleep

Tiredness sleep doesn’t fix, mornings getting harder, everything taking more effort than it should.

🧠
The harsh inner voice

Guilt, feeling like a burden, a running self-criticism you’d never accept aimed at anyone else.

😓
Withdrawing

Pulling back from friends and family without quite deciding to, and the loneliness that follows.

🌑
Numbness, not just sadness

Feeling flat or empty rather than tearful — the version of low mood that’s easiest to miss and dismiss.

🫀
Feeling stuck

Drift after a setback, a loss or a change — where low mood and feeling directionless feed each other.

What the pressure looks like here

Delhi puts two things in the same few kilometres: a lot of very selective institutions, and a lot of families who moved here so their child could get into one. DU cut-offs, the CUET scramble, the civil services coaching belts around Mukherjee Nagar and Rajinder Nagar, NEET and JEE hanging over everything.

Two stories come up over and over in sessions. One is the aspirant in a rented room with no family nearby, where a bad month has nobody in it. The isolation does more harm than the syllabus ever does. The other is the student whose family has visibly given things up to fund this, who has quietly decided that struggling would be ungrateful. So they say nothing.

What’s already available in Delhi, and what’s missing

Delhi is better served than most cities. Psychiatry at AIIMS, and IHBAS in Dilshad Garden, one of the big public mental health institutions in the country. If you need a psychiatric assessment, or things are severe, go there.

Neither is built for the much larger group who aren’t ill but are struggling anyway. The student who can’t make themselves open the book. The aspirant on a third attempt. The twenty-six-year-old who has stopped enjoying anything and can’t say why. Public psychiatry has waiting lists and a clinical threshold you’ve to cross first. Private therapy here runs ₹1,500 to ₹3,000 a session, which for a student means asking a parent for the money, which for a lot of them means not going.

What this is, and what it isn’t

It’s counselling support, not treatment: confidential conversations about what you’re carrying and what might help. Frequently asked questions

Do I have to travel anywhere in Delhi for a session?

No. Sessions are online by video, voice or chat, so there’s no clinic to reach across the city and no waiting room to sit in. For most people in Delhi that’s the point — the traffic is a real barrier, and so is being seen.

Is this available in Hindi?

Yes. Hindi or English, whichever you actually think in. For a lot of people in Delhi, describing what’s going on is much easier in Hindi even when their working life runs in English.

I am preparing for UPSC and can’t afford time away. Is one session useful?

Yes, and one is genuinely a reasonable amount to start with. Forty-five minutes, online, at a slot you pick. A great deal of aspirant distress is about isolation and the arithmetic of attempts rather than about the syllabus, and that’s workable in a single conversation.

Will my parents find out?

No. Nothing is reported to your parents, and you can book using a first name only. This is the most common question we are asked in Delhi and the answer doesn’t have caveats attached to it, beyond the standard limit where there’s serious risk to life.

Can counselling help with depression?

Often, yes — especially for mild to moderate low mood, where counselling, support and small changes make a real difference. More severe depression sometimes needs medication alongside, and a counsellor will tell you honestly.

I feel numb rather than sad. Is that still worth talking about?

Yes. Loss of feeling — not enjoying anything, feeling flat or empty — is a core feature of depression and is often missed. It’s very much worth talking through.
