Understanding Overthinking

What overthinking actually is

Overthinking is thinking that has stopped doing its job. Healthy thinking is aimed: it works a problem, lands on a decision or an action, and switches off. Overthinking keeps the engine running after the destination has gone — it circles, repeats and branches, generating the feeling of effort without the result.

It usually takes one of two shapes. Rumination points backwards: replaying a conversation, a mistake, a “why did I say that.” Worry points forwards: rehearsing everything that could go wrong, the endless “what if.” Both share one trick — they masquerade as problem-solving, so you keep doing them, believing that one more lap will finally crack it.

It rarely does. The honest test is simple: after you’ve been turning something over for a while, are you closer to a decision or an action? If yes, that’s thinking. If you’re just more tangled and more tense, that’s overthinking — and the way out isn’t a better thought, it’s a different move.

Signs you’re in the loop

Everyone over-analyses now and then. It’s worth noticing when it becomes the default setting. You won’t have all of these, and a few doesn’t mean anything is wrong — it’s the pattern that matters.

🔄
Replaying & rehearsing

Running old conversations on a loop, or pre-living future ones that haven’t happened and may never.

🧾
Decisions stall

Even small choices feel heavy. You research, compare, ask everyone — and still can’t commit.

🌙
Worst at night

The mind refuses to power down at bedtime. The thoughts feel urgent and true at 1am and smaller by morning.

💪
Tense & drained

Tight shoulders, a busy head, tiredness that sleep doesn’t fix, trouble being present with people in front of you.

Why the mind gets stuck here

Overthinking feels safe, which is exactly why it’s so sticky. As long as you’re analysing, it feels like you’re in control and doing something about the problem — so the brain keeps choosing it over the discomfort of acting or letting go. It’s a strategy that delivers relief in the moment and nothing in the long run.

It’s also fed by a belief most overthinkers hold without noticing: if I think hard enough, I can be certain, and certainty will keep me safe. But most of life doesn’t offer certainty, so the thinking never completes — there’s always one more angle. The loop isn’t a sign you’re bad at thinking. It’s a sign you’re chasing a kind of certainty the situation can’t give.

A few questions to sit with

There are no right answers here. These are prompts to catch the loop in the act — try them next time you notice yourself spinning.

  • Am I trying to solve a problem, or just trying to feel certain?
  • Is there an actual decision or action here — or nothing I can do right now?
  • What would I tell a friend who was stuck thinking exactly this?
  • Will this matter in a week? In a year?
  • If I had to act in the next ten minutes, what would “good enough” look like?

What actually helps

None of this replaces proper support, but all of it is worth doing while you decide:

  • Turn thoughts into actions or bin them. For each worry, ask: is there a step I can take? If yes, write the step and do it. If no, there’s nothing to think about — the thinking is just friction. Naming “this isn’t actionable” is itself a release.
  • Schedule your worry. Set aside 15 minutes a day to overthink on purpose, on paper. When a thought shows up outside that window, note it for the slot. Oddly, most thoughts lose their grip once they know they’ll get their turn.
  • Move your body out of the loop. Overthinking lives in stillness. A brisk walk, a cold splash of water, ten minutes of anything physical interrupts the circuit faster than any clever counter-thought.
  • Set a “good enough” bar before you decide. Decide in advance what matters and how long you’ll take. Most decisions don’t improve past a point; extra thinking only adds doubt.
  • Get it out of your head. Write the thought down or say it aloud to someone. Externalising it shrinks it — a worry trapped inside sounds enormous; the same worry on paper is usually smaller and more manageable than it felt.
  • Practise “this is a thought, not a fact.” You don’t have to argue with or obey every thought. Notice it, label it — “there’s the replay again” — and let it pass without climbing aboard.

When to get support

Self-help works well while overthinking is still an occasional nuisance. It works less well once it’s running your days — stealing your sleep, freezing your decisions, or wrapped up with anxiety or low mood that won’t shift. If you’ve genuinely tried to step out and keep getting pulled back, that’s not a willpower failure; it’s a sign the loop is anchored to something underneath.

That’s where talking to someone qualified helps most. Overthinking is very hard to out-think from the inside, because the tool you’d use to escape it is the same tool that’s stuck. A counsellor gives you an outside view and the specific skills to loosen it.

Common myths, cleared up

“Overthinking means I’m being responsible.”

Responsibility is deciding and acting. Overthinking is the thing that stops you doing either, dressed up as diligence. You can care deeply about something and still be stuck.

“If I stop analysing, I’ll make a mistake.”

Past a point, more analysis doesn’t reduce mistakes — it just reduces confidence. Most good decisions are made with incomplete information and corrected along the way.

“My thoughts at night are the real truth.”

Night thoughts aren’t wiser, just louder, because nothing else is competing for your attention and a tired brain is bad at shutting worry down. Judge them again in daylight.

Frequently asked questions

Is overthinking the same as thinking carefully?

No. Useful thinking moves towards a decision and then stops. Overthinking circles the same ground without reaching one — it feels productive but produces only more thinking. A good test: after ten minutes, are you any closer to an answer, or just more tangled?

Why do I overthink the most at night?

At night the distractions that normally keep thoughts at bay — work, people, tasks — drop away, and a tired brain is worse at shutting a worry down. The thoughts aren’t more true at 1am; they’re just louder because nothing else is competing for the stage.

How do I stop overthinking a decision?

Set a deadline and a “good enough” bar before you start, because most decisions don’t improve past a point — extra thinking just adds doubt. Write the two or three things that actually matter, pick the option that best fits them, and accept that you’ll never have complete information. Done and slightly imperfect beats endlessly analysed.

When should I get help for overthinking?

When it’s costing you sleep, focus or decisions, when it’s tied to anxiety or low mood, or when you’ve tried to stop and can’t. Overthinking is often the surface of something a counsellor can help with directly, and talking it through with someone outside the loop breaks it faster than trying to out-think it alone.
