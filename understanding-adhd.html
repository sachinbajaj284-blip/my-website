What ADHD actually is

ADHD — attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder — is a neurodevelopmental condition, meaning it’s a difference in how the brain develops and regulates attention, impulse and activity. It’s present from childhood, though in many people — especially girls and quieter kids — it’s missed until adulthood, when the demands of college, work or running a life outgrow the coping tricks that used to paper over it.

The old picture of ADHD — a hyperactive boy who can’t sit still — is only one version. Many adults and students have the inattentive kind: no obvious hyperactivity, just a mind that drifts, a desk that’s chaos, deadlines that arrive like ambushes, and a long history of “so much potential, if only they’d apply themselves.”

Importantly, ADHD is not a measure of intelligence or effort, and it’s not something a website can diagnose. A proper diagnosis needs a qualified professional (a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist) looking at your history from childhood onward. This page is to help you recognise the pattern and know what to do next — not to label you.

Signs to look for

ADHD shows up across attention, impulse and activity — and almost everyone has some of these sometimes. What points toward ADHD is the pattern being lifelong, present in more than one area of life (study, work, home), and causing real difficulty. Only an assessment can confirm it.

🌠
Inattention

Easily distracted, trouble finishing things, losing things, forgetting appointments, reading a page and taking in none of it, jumping between tasks without completing any.

⚡
Impulsivity

Blurting things out, interrupting, acting on an idea before thinking it through, impulsive spending, difficulty waiting or sitting through things.

🏃
Restlessness

An inner engine that won’t idle, fidgeting, needing to move, feeling on edge when still — which in adults is often felt internally rather than seen.

💤
The knock-on effects

Procrastination and last-minute panic, time blindness, emotional ups and downs, low self-esteem from years of “not living up to potential”, and burnout from overcompensating.

What causes it, and what keeps it going

ADHD is strongly heritable — it runs in families — and reflects real, well-documented differences in brain development and in the systems that manage attention, motivation and impulse. It is not caused by bad parenting, too much screen time, laziness or sugar, however often those get blamed.

What makes ADHD easier or harder to live with is shaped by environment: structure, interest, sleep, stress and demands all change how much it gets in the way. That’s why the same person can hyper-focus effortlessly on something they love and be unable to start something they find dull — and why the right strategies and support make a genuine difference.

What actually helps

None of this replaces proper support, but all of it is worth doing while you decide:

  • Get a proper assessment first. Because ADHD is often confused with anxiety, depression or just stress, and because treatment follows diagnosis, the single most useful step is an assessment by a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist. A counsellor can help you decide whether to pursue one.
  • Externalise everything. Don’t rely on an ADHD brain to remember — get it out of your head and into lists, alarms, calendars and visible reminders. The system does the remembering so you don’t have to.
  • Shrink the first step. Starting is the hard part. Break tasks down until the first step is almost embarrassingly small, and let momentum do the rest.
  • Use structure and deadlines deliberately. ADHD brains run on interest and urgency. Body-doubling (working alongside someone), short timers and real external deadlines turn vague tasks into ones you can actually start.
  • Treat the co-travellers. Years of struggle often leave anxiety, low mood and low self-worth alongside the ADHD. Those are very workable with support, and addressing them helps whether or not an ADHD diagnosis is confirmed.

When to get support

If you recognise a lifelong pattern that’s genuinely holding back your studies, work, relationships or sense of yourself, it’s worth pursuing a proper assessment rather than wondering. A diagnosis can be a relief — it reframes years of “why can’t I just…” as something real and workable — and it opens the door to strategies and, where appropriate, treatment.

Common myths, cleared up

“ADHD is just kids who can’t sit still.”

ADHD continues into adulthood for most people who have it, and often looks nothing like hyperactivity — it shows up as disorganisation, procrastination, forgetfulness and an restless mind. Many adults are diagnosed only after a lifetime of struggling quietly.

“Everyone’s a bit ADHD these days.”

Everyone gets distracted, but ADHD is a persistent, lifelong pattern that significantly disrupts daily life across settings. Trivialising it makes it harder for people who genuinely have it to be taken seriously and get help.

“If you were smart or tried harder, you’d be fine.”

ADHD has nothing to do with intelligence, and the problem isn’t a shortage of trying — many people with ADHD are exhausted precisely from trying twice as hard to keep up. It’s a regulation difference, not a character flaw.

Frequently asked questions

Can an online test diagnose ADHD?

No. Online checklists can raise the question, but ADHD can only be diagnosed by a qualified professional — usually a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist — who takes a full history going back to childhood and rules out other explanations. That’s why there’s no ADHD quiz on this page.

Can you develop ADHD as an adult?

ADHD begins in childhood by definition, even when it’s recognised only in adulthood. What often happens is that someone copes well enough until the demands of adult life — study, work, managing a household — outstrip their coping strategies, and the long-standing pattern finally becomes visible.

Is ADHD related to anxiety and depression?

Very often, yes. Living with undiagnosed ADHD — and years of falling short of expectations — frequently leads to anxiety, low mood and low self-esteem. These can be worked on with counselling, and treating them helps a great deal, whether or not an ADHD diagnosis is eventually confirmed.

Can a counsellor help with ADHD?

A counsellor doesn’t diagnose ADHD, but can help you understand the pattern, build practical systems, manage the anxiety and self-criticism that ride with it, and guide you toward a formal assessment with a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist where that’s the right step.
