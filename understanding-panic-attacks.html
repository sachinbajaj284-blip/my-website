What a panic attack actually is

A panic attack is a sudden, intense wave of fear that comes with a rush of physical symptoms — a racing or pounding heart, tight chest, breathlessness, dizziness, trembling, sweating, tingling, and a frightening sense that something terrible is about to happen or that you’re losing control. It can come out of the blue or be triggered by a situation.

Here is the key fact: a panic attack is your body’s emergency response (the “fight or flight” system) firing when there’s no actual emergency. Every sensation — the heart, the breath, the dizziness — is that system doing exactly what it’s built to do to help you flee danger. It feels catastrophic, but it is not harming you, and it cannot keep going; the body physically cannot sustain the surge, so it peaks and comes down, usually within minutes.

When panic attacks keep happening, and you start living in fear of the next one — avoiding places where one might strike — that’s what’s called panic disorder. The fear of the fear becomes its own problem, and often a bigger one than the attacks themselves.

Signs to look for

Panic attacks peak fast — usually within about ten minutes — and then subside. The symptoms are overwhelmingly physical, which is exactly why so many people believe they’re having a medical emergency the first time.

💓
Heart & chest

Pounding, racing or skipping heartbeat; chest tightness or pain — the symptom most often mistaken for a heart attack.

💨
Breathing

Shortness of breath, a feeling of choking or smothering, or breathing so fast it makes the dizziness worse.

😵
Head & senses

Dizziness, light-headedness, feeling unreal or detached, blurred vision, a sense of being about to faint.

🪯
Body & dread

Sweating, trembling, tingling or numbness in the hands or face, hot or cold flushes, and an overwhelming fear of dying, losing control or “going mad”.

What causes it, and what keeps it going

Panic attacks often arrive in periods of high stress, after a big change, or on a background of ongoing anxiety — but the first one can seem to come from nowhere. Once you’ve had one, a second mechanism takes over: you become hyper-alert to normal body sensations, read a racing heart or a dizzy moment as the start of another attack, and that very alarm sets one off. The fear of panic becomes the cause of panic.

This is good news in disguise, because it means the most powerful thing you can change is your reaction to the sensations. When you stop treating a pounding heart as an emergency, you cut the loop that keeps the attacks coming.

What actually helps

None of this replaces proper support, but all of it is worth doing while you decide:

  • Don’t fight it — let it peak. Remind yourself: this is a panic attack, it will peak and pass, and it is not harming me. Fighting the symptoms adds fear and fuel. Letting the wave rise and fall is what shortens it.
  • Slow the out-breath. Panic speeds your breathing, which worsens the dizziness and tingling. Breathe out slowly — longer out than in, say four in and six out — to settle the nervous system. This is the single most useful thing in the moment.
  • Ground yourself in the room. Name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can touch. It pulls attention out of the body’s alarm and back into the here-and-now, where nothing is actually wrong.
  • Stay where you are if you safely can. Fleeing the place teaches your brain the place was dangerous and makes avoidance grow. Riding it out where it struck, when it’s safe to, is how the fear shrinks.
  • Lower the background. Less caffeine, better sleep, regular movement and addressing ongoing stress all reduce how primed your system is to fire in the first place.

When to get support

One panic attack in a stressful stretch is unpleasant but not unusual. It’s worth getting support when they’re recurring, when you’ve started avoiding places or situations in case one strikes, or when the fear of the next attack is shrinking your life. Panic disorder responds very well to the right help, often quickly.

One important note: the first time, or if symptoms are unusual for you, it’s sensible to get chest pain and breathlessness checked by a doctor to rule out a physical cause. Once that’s clear, a counsellor can help you break the panic cycle for good.

Common myths, cleared up

“A panic attack could give me a heart attack or kill me.”

A panic attack is frightening but not physically dangerous. The racing heart and chest tightness are your alarm system, not your heart failing. It peaks and passes on its own. (If you’ve never had chest symptoms checked, do see a doctor once to be sure of the cause.)

“If I have a panic attack in public, I’ll faint or lose control.”

Fainting is actually unlikely in panic, because your blood pressure rises rather than drops. And people very rarely “lose control” or act out — the sense of impending disaster is a symptom, not a prediction.

“Avoiding the places it happens will keep me safe.”

Avoidance is what turns occasional panic into panic disorder. Each place you avoid, your world gets a little smaller and the fear gets a little bigger. Facing them gradually, with support, is what reverses it.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a panic attack last?

Most panic attacks peak within about ten minutes and ease off soon after, though you may feel shaky or drained for a while afterward. The intensity can make it feel much longer. The body simply cannot sustain the surge, which is why it always comes down.

How is a panic attack different from anxiety?

Anxiety tends to build gradually and can simmer for hours or days. A panic attack is sudden and intense, peaking fast with strong physical symptoms, then subsiding. Someone can have anxiety without panic attacks, panic attacks without much day-to-day anxiety, or both.

How do I stop a panic attack when it’s happening?

You can’t force it to stop, but you can stop feeding it: remind yourself it’s panic and it will pass, slow your out-breath, ground yourself in your surroundings, and avoid fleeing if you’re safe. The attack peaks and falls on its own; your job is not to add fear to it.

Should I see a doctor about panic attacks?

If it’s your first, or the symptoms are new or unusual, it’s wise to have chest pain and breathlessness checked once to rule out a physical cause. If attacks keep happening or you’re living in fear of them, counselling helps break the cycle, and is often quite effective.
