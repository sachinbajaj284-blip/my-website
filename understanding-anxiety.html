What anxiety actually is

Anxiety is your body’s threat-response doing its job at the wrong time. A racing heart, tight chest, a mind that leaps to the worst outcome — that machinery exists to keep you safe from real danger. It becomes a problem when it fires over things that aren’t dangerous, fires too often, and won’t settle once it has.

Ordinary worry has an object and an end: you worry about an exam, you sit the exam, the worry fades. Anxiety is worry that outlasts its cause, jumps from one thing to the next, and keeps the body switched on in between. The thought “what if” arrives before anything has happened, and leaves only when the next “what if” takes over.

Clinically, a counsellor or doctor might call a persistent version of this generalised anxiety; worry tied to specific situations has other names. But you do not need a label to take it seriously. If the bracing has become the background hum of your day, that is worth understanding.

Signs to look for

Anxiety shows up in the body, the mind and in what you do — often in all three at once. You won’t have every one of these, and having some doesn’t mean you have an anxiety disorder. It’s the pattern and how long it lasts that matter.

💓
In the body

Racing or pounding heart, tight chest, shallow breath, a knotted stomach, restlessness, trouble sleeping, muscles that stay tense even at rest.

🧠
In the mind

Worry that jumps from topic to topic, catastrophising, replaying conversations, a constant sense that something bad is about to happen.

🏃
In what you do

Avoiding places or people, checking and re-checking, seeking reassurance, procrastinating on the thing you’re anxious about, staying endlessly “busy”.

💤
In your energy

Tiredness that sleep doesn’t fix, trouble concentrating, irritability, feeling on edge and exhausted at the same time.

What causes it, and what keeps it going

Anxiety rarely has one cause. It usually grows where a few things meet: a temperament that runs a little more alert than average, a stretch of real pressure (exams, money, a strained home, a new city), and habits that quietly feed it — too little sleep, too much caffeine, endless scrolling, and avoiding whatever you’re afraid of, which teaches the brain the fear was justified.

That last one matters most, because it’s the part you can change. Avoidance feels like relief in the moment and makes anxiety stronger over time. Most of what helps works by gently reversing it.

What actually helps

None of this replaces proper support, but all of it is worth doing while you decide:

  • Slow the breath out, not in. A longer out-breath than in-breath (try four counts in, six out) tells the nervous system the threat has passed. It’s the fastest switch you have, and it works in exam halls and meetings.
  • Name it, don’t fight it. “This is anxiety, not danger” sounds too simple to work, but labelling the feeling takes the heat out of it. Fighting it or telling yourself to stop usually makes it louder.
  • Approach, in small steps. Whatever you’ve been avoiding, do a smaller version of it today. Anxiety shrinks when you prove to yourself the thing was survivable. This is the single most effective move there is.
  • Cut the fuel. Less caffeine, more sleep, less late-night scrolling. None of these cure anxiety, but all of them lower the baseline so everything else has a chance to work.
  • Put worry in a box. Set aside 15 minutes a day to worry on purpose, on paper. When a worry shows up outside that window, note it for later. Worry hates a schedule.

When to get support

Self-help works well while anxiety is still something you can get some distance from. It works less well once it’s steering your days — when you’re turning down things you want to do, when sleep has gone for weeks, when the worry has spread to almost everything, or when the physical symptoms are frightening you in their own right.

That’s the point where talking to someone qualified stops being an overreaction and becomes the quicker route. Anxiety is very convincing from the inside, and very hard to see straight on your own.

Common myths, cleared up

“It’s just overthinking — I should be able to stop.”

Anxiety is a physical response, not a bad habit of thought. Willpower doesn’t switch off a racing heart. What works is changing what the body and brain are reacting to, which is a skill, not a matter of trying harder.

“If I avoid what scares me, I’ll feel better.”

Avoidance is the engine of anxiety. Every time you dodge the feared thing, the fear grows. Relief now, bigger problem later.

“Everyone’s anxious these days, so it’s normal.”

Common isn’t the same as fine. If anxiety is costing you sleep, focus, relationships or things you’d otherwise do, it’s worth addressing — however many other people are carrying it too.

Frequently asked questions

What’s the difference between anxiety and an anxiety disorder?

Anxiety is a normal emotion everyone feels. It becomes a disorder, in clinical terms, when it’s frequent, hard to control, out of proportion to the situation and getting in the way of ordinary life — and when that’s been true for weeks or months. Only a qualified professional can make that call; a self-check can only tell you roughly how heavy things have become.

Is anxiety curable?

Anxiety is highly manageable. Most people don’t eliminate it entirely — some anxiety is useful — but they learn to turn the volume down so it stops running their decisions. For many people the change is large.

Can anxiety cause physical symptoms?

Yes, and they can be intense: chest tightness, breathlessness, a pounding heart, dizziness, stomach trouble. These are real, not imagined. If symptoms are new or severe it’s sensible to rule out physical causes with a doctor first.

Should I see a counsellor or a psychiatrist for anxiety?

Many people start with counselling, which helps you understand what keeps the anxiety going and build skills to lower it. If anxiety is severe, or counselling alone isn’t enough, a psychiatrist can assess whether medication would help. A counsellor will tell you honestly if that’s the direction to go.
