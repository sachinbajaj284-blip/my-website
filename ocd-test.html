What your score means

Eighteen items scored out of 72. Broad bands:

  • 0–20 — below the threshold. Below the usual screening cut-off. Occasional checking or intrusive thoughts are part of normal life.
  • 21–40 — raised. Around or above the usual cut-off of 21. Worth assessing properly, especially if rituals are eating time.
  • 41–72 — high. A heavy load. OCD-specific therapy is genuinely worth arranging rather than trying to push through.
A more useful question than the number: how much time a day do the thoughts and rituals take — and what do you avoid because of them? That's the clearest sign OCD is costing you something.

Intrusive thoughts aren't the problem

Almost everyone gets unwanted, disturbing thoughts — violent, sexual, blasphemous, whatever your mind can conjure. In OCD the thought isn't the illness; the struggle against it is. The more you try to neutralise, check or reassure the thought away, the louder it gets. This is exactly what the right therapy targets, and why fighting the thoughts harder never works.

What actually helps with OCD

OCD needs specific handling — some generic advice even makes it worse. These point the right way:

  • Resist the ritual, not the thought. You can't control which thoughts arrive, but you can choose not to perform the checking, washing or reassurance. Each time you don't, the anxiety drops on its own — this is the core of ERP.
  • Stop seeking reassurance. Asking 'are you sure it's fine?' or Googling symptoms feels like relief but is a compulsion. It teaches your brain the fear was valid.
  • Let the doubt sit. OCD demands certainty it can never get. Practising tolerating 'maybe' is more useful than any amount of checking.
  • Don't fight thoughts with logic. Arguing with an intrusive thought keeps you engaged with it. Noticing it and letting it pass works better than debating it.
  • Get the right therapy. OCD responds specifically to Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP). General talk therapy alone usually isn't enough — this is worth knowing before you start.

When self-help isn't enough

Self-help has real limits with OCD, because the condition is good at turning coping strategies into new rituals. If intrusive thoughts and compulsions are taking real time out of your day, or you're avoiding things to keep them at bay, that's the stage to get proper support. OCD responds specifically and well to ERP therapy — which is hard to do alone precisely because it means deliberately not doing the thing that brings relief. A counselling conversation is a good first step to find the right help.

Frequently asked questions

Is this test free and private?

Completely. No sign-up, no email, no payment. Your answers aren't stored, sent or seen by anyone — they stay in your browser for the length of the check and are gone when you close the tab.

Does this diagnose OCD?

No. The OCI-R is a screening scale, not a diagnostic instrument. It tells you whether obsessive-compulsive symptoms are worth assessing — only a qualified clinician can diagnose OCD.

I get horrible intrusive thoughts. Does that mean I'm a bad person?

No. Intrusive thoughts are common, and in OCD they're the opposite of your values — that's exactly why they distress you so much. The thought is not an intention, and having it doesn't make it true.

What is the OCI-R?

The Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory–Revised is an eighteen-item questionnaire covering washing, checking, ordering, obsessing, hoarding and mental neutralising, each scored 0–4, for a total out of 72. Around 21 or above often indicates OCD.

Is it anonymous?
