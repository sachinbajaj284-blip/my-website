What counselling for low mood can help with in Mumbai

You don’t need a diagnosis or a reason that looks big enough from outside. These are the things people bring when low mood has been weighing on them:

🌁
Loss of interest

Things you used to enjoy going flat — hobbies, people, plans. Often the earliest and most honest sign.

😶
Exhaustion & sleep

Tiredness sleep doesn’t fix, mornings getting harder, everything taking more effort than it should.

🧠
The harsh inner voice

Guilt, feeling like a burden, a running self-criticism you’d never accept aimed at anyone else.

😓
Withdrawing

Pulling back from friends and family without quite deciding to, and the loneliness that follows.

🌑
Numbness, not just sadness

Feeling flat or empty rather than tearful — the version of low mood that’s easiest to miss and dismiss.

🫀
Feeling stuck

Drift after a setback, a loss or a change — where low mood and feeling directionless feed each other.

What the pressure looks like here

What Mumbai adds is a shortage of time and a shortage of space. Two hours each way is a normal commute. A flat shared with three generations is a normal flat. Between them, a lot of people here have nowhere to be alone and not one hour that belongs to them. Distress that would surface somewhere else gets folded into a day with no room to notice it.

Then there’s the local script about coping. Mumbai’s picture of itself is a city that keeps going, and it does, and that’s worth something. It also slides easily into the belief that struggling means you personally lacked stamina. People turn up to a first session having already decided they should have managed this on their own.

For students it runs along the HSC and CET calendar, the medical and engineering funnel, and a tight cluster of colleges where the gap between two of them gets treated as the gap between two entire lives.

What’s already available in Mumbai, and what’s missing

Some of the best psychiatric care in India is in Mumbai’s public hospitals. KEM and Sion among them, and TISS has been serious about mental health work in this city for decades. If you need a psychiatric assessment, those are the right doors to knock on.

Harder to find is an unhurried conversation when you aren’t ill. Private therapy here is about the most expensive in the country, and public services quite rightly go to the people who are worst off. That leaves the middle, struggling but functioning, not in crisis, which is where most people actually live, with the least available to them.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to travel for a session?

No, and in Mumbai that’s often the deciding factor. Sessions are online by video, voice or chat, so a session costs you forty-five minutes rather than forty-five minutes plus three hours of travel.

I share a home and have no privacy. How do people manage a session?

Most commonly by voice or chat rather than video, with headphones, from wherever is workable — including a parked car or an office meeting room. It is a very common constraint here and it’s worth saying up front rather than deciding a session is impossible.

Can we talk in Marathi?

Sessions run in Hindi or English. If Marathi is the language you think in, it’s worth saying so at the start — a lot can be accommodated in how a session is run, and it is better to raise it than to work in a language that makes the difficult parts harder to reach.

Is ₹249 really the full cost of a first session?

Yes, for a 45-minute first session with code FIRST50; sessions after that are ₹499. Mumbai private therapy commonly runs several times that, which is exactly why this is priced the way it is.

Can counselling help with depression?

Often, yes — especially for mild to moderate low mood, where counselling, support and small changes make a real difference. More severe depression sometimes needs medication alongside, and a counsellor will tell you honestly.

I feel numb rather than sad. Is that still worth talking about?

Yes. Loss of feeling — not enjoying anything, feeling flat or empty — is a core feature of depression and is often missed. It’s very much worth talking through.
